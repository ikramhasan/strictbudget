Budgeting with credit cards comes down to one rule: a card purchase spends money you already have. In a zero based budget, the moment you buy something on a card, the amount leaves the spending category and moves to the card's payment category. When the bill arrives, the money to pay it is already there. Debt from before you started budgeting is the one part that isn't, so you pay it down on purpose, a set amount each month.

Here is how that worked for one household in October, with every number their budget showed.

## Two cards, one October

Nadia and Sam share one budget in strictbudget. They have two cards:

- **Juniper**, a Visa they pay in full. Groceries, fuel, dining out and streaming go on it. The statement closes on the last day of the month and is due on the 21st.
- **Harbor**, a Mastercard with $4,200.00 of debt they brought with them when they started budgeting in April. It charges 24.99% APR. They stopped using it and pay it down every month.

On October 1st, Juniper owed $820.91, all of September's spending, and its payment category held exactly $820.91. Harbor owed $2,400.00.

## Every card gets a payment category

When you add a credit card account, strictbudget adds a category with the card's name to the Credit Card Payments group. Its Available is the money set aside for that card. You don't assign to it for everyday spending. It fills as you spend.

A card's starting balance works differently. Harbor's $4,200.00 was debt they already owed, so it has no category (the register shows "Category not needed") and it didn't touch Ready to Assign. It showed up as debt the payment category didn't cover yet. Paying that down has [its own section](#paying-down-old-debt) below.

## Paying the bill

On the 21st they paid the September statement, $820.91, from Checking with Record Payment. A payment moves money between two of your own accounts, so it isn't spending. Checking went down by $820.91, and so did the card's balance and the payment category, from $1,356.76 to $535.85. Ready to Assign didn't move, because the spending was already counted when they paid with the card.

**Statement balance or current balance?** The statement balance is what the card asks for by the due date, and paying it in full is what keeps most cards from charging interest. The current balance also includes purchases made since the statement closed. On the 21st, Juniper's payment category held enough for both, so either payment was fine. They paid the statement and left October's spending for November's bill.

## Returns go back where they came from

On the 9th they bought shoes for $89.99 on Juniper and returned them on the 16th. They put the refund in Clothing, the category the purchase came from. Clothing went from $169.37 to $79.38 and back to $169.37. The payment category rose by $89.99 and fell back by the same amount, because the card no longer owed it.

## When a category runs out: credit overspending

Dining out had $120.00 for October. Three dinners used exactly that: $38.40, $27.15 and $54.45. On the 24th they had sushi for $24.50 on the card anyway.

With nothing left in Dining out, the $24.50 had no money behind it, so nothing moved to the payment category. Dining out showed −$24.50 in yellow, and Juniper's payment category held $682.01 for a balance of $706.51.

**Why yellow, and why it matters.** Overspending from checking or a debit card shows in red, and strictbudget takes it out of next month's Ready to Assign, because that money already left the bank. Overspending on a credit card shows in yellow. No money left the bank yet. The purchase became new debt, so instead of touching next month, the card's payment category stays short by that amount until you cover it. Leave it, and next month's bill is $24.50 bigger than what's set aside for it. This is how card balances creep up in a budget that otherwise looks fine.

<Tip title="Cover yellow before the month ends">
  A yellow category means some card spending has no money behind it. Move money to it, or if you can't, plan to pay it off as debt with a payment target.
</Tip>

## Interest gets its own category

strictbudget doesn't track a card's interest rate, so interest is spending like anything else. Nadia and Sam have a category called Interest & Fees with a target that refills it to $90.00 each month, a bit more than their largest charge so far ($87.47 in April).

To estimate a month's interest, multiply what you owe by the APR and divide by 12. On $2,400.00 at 24.99%, that's $2,400.00 × 0.2499 ÷ 12 = $49.98. Card issuers work from your average daily balance, so the real charge can be a little different.

The charge posted on the 3rd: $49.98, which they put in Interest & Fees. It's card spending, so $49.98 moved into Harbor's payment category, ready to be paid with the rest.

## Paying down old debt

Harbor's payment category has a target: Pay Specific Amount Monthly, $300.00. Each month they assign $300.00 to it, the interest arrives through Interest & Fees, and they pay the total. In October that was $300.00 + $49.98 = $349.98, paid on the 20th. Harbor went from $2,400.00 to $2,100.00.

**If you'd rather pick a date:** the other payment target, Pay Off Balance by Date, spreads what the card owes over the months left. For May 2027, that's $2,100.00 over seven months, $300.00 a month. It doesn't add interest, so keep an interest category next to it.

**Can you keep using a card you're paying down?** Yes, as long as the category has the money. That spending moves into the payment category the way Juniper's does, and you pay it on top of the debt payment. Nadia and Sam stopped using Harbor anyway, which keeps the two jobs apart.

## Questions people ask

### Should I pay the statement balance or the whole balance?

Paying the statement balance in full by the due date avoids interest on most cards. If the payment category holds more, you can pay more. The money is set aside either way.

### Where does cash back go?

If the reward comes as a statement credit, categorize it to Ready to Assign. The card then owes less while its payment category keeps what it had, so the category holds more than the card owes, and you can move the difference to any category. A $25.00 credit on Juniper would leave $706.51 set aside for $681.51 owed, with $25.00 to move.

### What if I paid more than I owed?

A card balance above zero is your money, like a small checking account. Spending from it counts as cash until the balance is back at zero, and only what goes past zero becomes debt.

### I already have card debt. Where do I start?

Add the card with today's balance. Give its payment category a target you can meet every month, and add an interest category for the charges. From then on, as long as every purchase is covered, the balance goes down each month by what you assign to the payment category.

### Is this how YNAB handles credit cards?

Yes. strictbudget uses the same method, with payment categories, credit overspending and payment targets, so the habits carry over if you're coming from YNAB. You can bring a YNAB budget over with Import on the budgets page.

## The habit

Before you pay with a card, look at the category, not the card's limit. If the category has the money, the bill is already covered. If it doesn't, move money first.
